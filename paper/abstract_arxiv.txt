A variational account of life proposes that living organisations maximise an information functional Phi that rewards prediction of the environment and memory and penalises complexity. We do not postulate Phi. We define the viability of a structure from the dynamics alone, as its mean first-passage time out of a set of typical states. Then we test whether information scores predict it. The tests run in seven pre-registered steps on four kinds of model system. These are random linear Ornstein-Uhlenbeck worlds in 9 parameter settings, inertial units with and without an energy-pumping drive, a linear homeostat with a hidden environmental state, and self-replicating Gray-Scott spots with heritable types and mutation. The original functional Phi_orig = P + C - lambda K_H does not predict viability: its rank correlation with log life time is positive, with a confidence interval above zero, in 3 of 9 settings only, and its complexity term depends on the units. Memory C (at equal size) and a unit-free variant Phi_TC = P + C - 0.25 TC (total correlation) predict viability in all 9 settings. Memory cannot tell drive-sustained structures from passive ones: at a long lag it rates passive bistable units slightly above driven oscillators (AUC 0.57 [0.51, 0.65]), while a flux cut separates the two classes without overlap. Predictive information pays only when an action loop uses it: breaking the sensor-actuator link shortens life about 3.5 times. In spot colonies we measure per-generation Price selection terms with a neutral control and a nonzero transmission term from copying errors, but no sharp error threshold. A numerical audit found a structure-dependent bias in the exit check and a filter that dropped 54% of merge pairs. The results above use the corrected code. So an arrow of organisation has partial support in these systems. No single information score is right in all test beds.
